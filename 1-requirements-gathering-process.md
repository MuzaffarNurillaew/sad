# Requirements Gathering Process

Two methods have been carried out: a semi-structured interview with the director, and direct observation of the main-slaughter process via the farm's own security cameras. A third method, a direct artifact review of the paper notebook and pDaftar screens, was deliberately not pursued: both contain the business's financial records, and the owner was not willing to share or photograph them for a university report.

A semi-structured interview, rather than a fixed questionnaire, fits this business because no written procedures exist anywhere on the farm — the director carries the whole operation in his own working memory, including cost structure, supplier terms, and feed schedules that are adjusted case by case. A rigid survey would miss the conditional logic (e.g. "if this factory's feed, then this many days") that only surfaces when a follow-up question is asked. Direct observation was used alongside the interview because the slaughter crew's work is something the director describes secondhand — he does not do the scalding or plucking himself — so camera footage of the crew in action checks the process independently of his account.

The interview was conducted on 2026-10-25, lasted 51 minutes, and began with an explicit verbal consent request, which the director agreed to.

On procurement, the interview confirmed and extended the contract basis already stated in the business case: chicks mostly come from Tashkent and the nearby Guliston valley, and the feed arrangement runs under a formal annual contract settled on roughly a 60-day credit cycle, paid at the end of the cycle rather than upfront per order — a detail the business case did not previously have.

On feed quality, the director linked timing to outcome in a way not previously documented: the better the quality of the factory supplying the first feed of a batch, the higher the resulting meat quality.

On pricing, the director gave a concrete recent example of how fast the market moves: a batch sold at roughly 27,500 UZS/kg had dropped to about 23,000 UZS/kg within 10 days. This matches the business case's framing of all three buyer prices as market-set, not farm-set, now with a real figure behind it.

On record-keeping, the director's own account goes further than the business case currently does: he wants pDaftar to separate figures by batch rather than pool them into one running total, and he was explicit that any future tool should be phone-based rather than desktop-based, since he spends most of his time away from any one fixed location.

The observation evidence is two clips recorded by the farm's own cameras on 2026-10-24, together covering roughly 24 minutes of a main-slaughter session, retained by the group. They show a crew of five to six workers at a fixed outdoor station: a wood-fired barrel heating water for scalding, a long table for plucking, and a stone basin where one worker carries out a separate cleaning task apart from the rest of the crew. This independently confirms the crew size and role division already documented in the business case, from the workers' own activity rather than the director's description of it, and adds a detail neither interview covered: the station is a fixed layout, not an improvised one, which matters for where any future check (e.g. a head count) would actually need to happen.

These are findings, not requirements: Section 7 traces its functional and non-functional requirements back to gaps like these; this section stops short of drafting them.

One thing remains open: the observation method captures the crew's real behavior directly rather than through the director's account, which is some evidence toward the brief's "prospective user" requirement, but it is passive — no crew member was interviewed or surveyed directly — so this should be read as a partial answer to that requirement, not a complete one. The artifact review is not a gap to close later — two methods with real, verifiable evidence already meet the rubric's stated minimum.
