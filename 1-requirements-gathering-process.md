# Requirements Gathering Process

Two methods have been carried out: a semi-structured interview with the director, and direct observation of the main-slaughter process via the farm's own security cameras. A third method, a direct artifact review of the paper notebook and pDaftar screens, was deliberately not pursued: both contain the business's financial records, and the owner was not willing to share or photograph them for a university report.

A semi-structured interview, rather than a fixed questionnaire, fits this business because no written procedures exist anywhere on the farm — the director carries the whole operation in his own working memory, including cost structure, supplier terms, and feed schedules that are adjusted case by case. A rigid survey would miss the conditional logic (e.g. "if this factory's feed, then this many days") that only surfaces when a follow-up question is asked. Direct observation was used alongside the interview because the slaughter crew's work is something the director describes secondhand — he does not do the scalding or plucking himself — so camera footage of the crew in action checks the process independently of his account.

The interview was conducted on 2026-10-25, lasted 51 minutes, and began with an explicit verbal consent request, which the director agreed to.

On procurement, the interview confirmed and extended the contract basis already stated in the business case: chicks mostly come from Tashkent and the nearby Guliston valley, and the feed arrangement runs under a formal annual contract settled on roughly a 60-day credit cycle, paid at the end of the cycle rather than upfront per order — a detail the business case did not previously have. Because only the contract itself is formal, the timing of each specific delivery is still arranged informally, with nothing recording whether a given delivery actually followed the agreed cycle.

On feed quality, the director linked timing to outcome in a way not previously documented: the better the quality of the factory supplying the first feed of a batch, the higher the resulting meat quality. Since which factory supplied which batch is never written down, this link can only be confirmed from memory, batch by batch.

On pricing, the director gave a concrete recent example of how fast the market moves: a batch sold at roughly 27,500 UZS/kg had dropped to about 23,000 UZS/kg within 10 days. This matches the business case's framing of all three buyer prices as market-set, not farm-set, now with a real figure behind it. Because no price history is kept anywhere, a swing like this is only ever recognized after the fact, from memory — one of the clearest gaps this interview surfaced.

On record-keeping, the director's own account goes further than the business case currently does: pDaftar is a generic bookkeeping app, not built for poultry farming, so getting it to separate figures by batch takes manual categorization tweaks rather than a feature it offers natively; he was also explicit that any future tool should be phone-based rather than desktop-based, since he spends most of his time away from any one fixed location. Because that separation depends on this manual upkeep rather than being automatic, a batch's figures can still end up pooled with another's whenever the categorization lapses. He raised voice input as the fix he'd actually want: small, incidental costs go unrecorded today because assigning a category, date, and reason by hand takes more effort than he has in the moment, while speaking the cost aloud and having it parsed automatically into the right fields would not.

The director also named monitoring and automation gaps beyond record-keeping itself. He wants each feed delivery tied to the specific supplier it came from, since he already ties a supplier's quality to the resulting meat quality but has no record connecting the two. He wants real-time temperature and humidity readings from each house and remote control of each house's lighting from his phone, instead of relying on manual checks and physical switches. He also wants an estimate of flock density to help decide slaughter or feeding timing, rather than the by-eye judgment the business case already describes. None of this is captured by the cameras already installed, which are used only for live viewing today, not for recording any of these readings or decisions.

The observation evidence is two clips recorded by the farm's own cameras on 2026-10-24, together covering roughly 24 minutes of a main-slaughter session, retained by the group. They show a crew of five to six workers at a fixed outdoor station: a wood-fired barrel heating water for scalding, a long table for plucking, and a stone basin where two to three workers carry out the cleaning task apart from the rest of the crew. This independently confirms the crew size and role division already documented in the business case, from the workers' own activity rather than the director's description of it, and adds a detail neither interview covered: the station is a fixed layout, not an improvised one. Because the layout stays the same from session to session, a future head-count check has exactly one place to look, rather than needing to find wherever the crew happened to set up that day.

These are findings, not requirements: Section 7 traces its functional and non-functional requirements back to gaps like these; this section stops short of drafting them.
