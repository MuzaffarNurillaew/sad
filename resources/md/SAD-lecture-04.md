What is a requirement?
Something that the product must do or a quality that the product must have.
1. Functional requirements – are things the product must do.
Examples: Ability to create a new blog post, edit existing posts, allow users to login, etc.
2. Non-functional requirements – are the qualities the product must have.
Examples: Prevailing color must be our corporate color, website should be able to process 100
concurrent users, interface must be easy to use, etc.
3. Constraints – are global issues that shape the requirements.
Examples: Must be available in TAS-IX only, product must comply with ISO XXXX standard, legal
constraints, etc.
NOTE: Collected/documented requirements must be unambiguous and non-vague.

Business Information Systems 13

1. Project Blast-off
• Is a short burst of activity that puts together all the pieces that are
necessary to get your project off to flying start and to ensure that your
project is viable and well founded.
• Determines the precise purpose that the product is to fulfil.
Deliverables:
• Purpose of the project • Constraints
• The client • Relevant facts and assumptions
• The customer • The scope of the work
• The stakeholders • The estimated cost
• The users • The risks
• Names
Business Information Systems 14

2. Trawling for Knowledge
1. Studying the work that is currently done in response to a business event.
2. Learning the desires and aspirations of the adjacent systems involved in
the business event.
3. Determining the best response that the organization can make to the
business event.
4. Determining the way that the product will contribute to the desired
response.
5. Determining and describing the requirements for the product.
Business Information Systems 15

3. Prototyping & scenario modelling
• The purpose of the prototype is to simulate aspects of the product and to
make it real enough to help users think of requirements that might
otherwise be missed.
• Scenario modelling is used to illustrate a situation. Contains a number of
scenes or episodes that tell a detailed story of a specific situation.
Business Information Systems 16

4. Writing down
• The process of documenting the requirements.
• Does not have to be “officially” documented. As far as it is clear and easy
to understand.
Business Information Systems 17

5. Quality Gateway
The process of filtering out non-feasible, incorrect or any requirement that
does not fit your definition of quality requirement.
Robertson, J. and Robertson, S (2012). Mastering the Requirements Process. Addison-Wesley.
Business Information Systems 18

6. Reusing requirements
• Entails making use of requirements that have been written for other
projects.
• They can come from a number of sources:
• a reuse library of specifications
• other requirements specifications that are similar or in the same
domain
• informally from other people’s experience.
Business Information Systems 19

7. Taking stock of the specification
• Considers all of the requirements and their effect on each.
• The objective of the stock-take is to review the specification and find
requirements that are:
• Missing
• Conflicting
• Ambiguous
Business Information Systems 20

8. Post Mortem
• Self-reflection and post mortem analysis.
• Questions you might want to ask:
• What did we do right?
• What did we do wrong?
• If we had to do it again, what would you do differently?
Business Information Systems 21

9. Tailoring your process
• Once you understand the content and purpose of each deliverable, ask
how each one would best be produced within your project environment
using your sources.
• The tailoring task is determining how your project will produce them.
